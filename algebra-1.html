FIRST — VOCABULARY

What a polynomial is made of

Before you can do anything to a polynomial, you need to be able to read one. A polynomial is just a sum of terms, where every term is a number times x raised to a whole, positive power. Click each piece below to see what it's called and why it matters.

Tap a term above to see what it's called ↑
degree = the highest power of x you can find
degree 1 → linear · degree 2 → quadratic · degree 3 → cubic
2 terms → binomial · 3 terms → trinomial

Why the degree matters: it tells you at a glance how "complicated" an expression is, and later in the course it tells you how many solutions an equation can have. Get comfortable spotting it before moving on.

STEP 1 OF 5

Addition & subtraction

The process here is always the same, in this order: (1) remove every bracket by multiplying out, (2) group terms that match, (3) add or subtract the matching terms. Terms only "match" — are like terms — if they have exactly the same power of x.

WORKED EXAMPLE
Expand and simplify: 7(x³ + 2x² − 5x) − 2(2 + 3x + 4x² − 2x³)
1
7(x³+2x²−5x) → 7x³ + 14x² − 35x
Multiply the 7 into every term inside its bracket, one at a time.
2
−2(2+3x+4x²−2x³) → −4 − 6x − 8x² + 4x³
Do the same with the −2. Careful with signs: −2 × −2x³ becomes +4x³.
3
7x³+14x²−35x −4−6x−8x²+4x³
Write everything out in one line now that both brackets are gone.
4
= 11x³ + 6x² − 41x − 4
Group and combine: (7x³+4x³)=11x³, (14x²−8x²)=6x², (−35x−6x)=−41x, and the lone constant −4 stays as is.
Self-check: simplify 3x²(4x² − 5x + 6) + 4x(8x³ − 2x − 3)
Step 1: 3x²(4x²−5x+6) = 12x⁴ − 15x³ + 18x²
Step 2: 4x(8x³−2x−3) = 32x⁴ − 8x² − 12x
Step 3: combine → 44x⁴ − 15x³ + 10x² − 12x

STEP 2 OF 5

Multiplying polynomial expressions

This is the distributive law taken further: a(b + c) = ab + ac. When both brackets have more than one term, split the first bracket into its separate pieces, and multiply each piece by the whole second bracket in turn. This is called expanding.

WORKED EXAMPLE
Simplify: (x − 5)(2x² − 3x + 6)
1
Split the first bracket: x and −5
Treat (x − 5) as two separate multipliers that will each hit the second bracket.
2
x(2x² − 3x + 6) = 2x³ − 3x² + 6x
Multiply the first piece, x, by every term in the second bracket.
3
−5(2x² − 3x + 6) = −10x² + 15x − 30
Now multiply the second piece, −5, by every term in the second bracket. A negative times a negative gives a positive — that's why −3x becomes +15x.
4
2x³ − 3x² + 6x − 10x² + 15x − 30
Write both results together in one line.
5
= 2x³ − 13x² + 21x − 30
Combine like terms: (−3x²−10x²)=−13x², (6x+15x)=21x. Nothing else matches, so the answer is done.
Self-check: expand (x + 4)(2x + 5)
Step 1: x(2x+5) = 2x²+5x
Step 2: 4(2x+5) = 8x+20
Step 3: combine → 2x²+13x+20

STEP 3 OF 5

Perfect squares

(x + a)² and (x − a)² always expand the same predictable way — once you recognise the pattern you never need to multiply it out longhand again.

(x + a)² = x² + 2ax + a²
(x − a)² = x² − 2ax + a²
WORKED EXAMPLE
Expand: (2x − 3)²
1
Match to (a − b)²: a = 2x, b = 3
Whatever is written first in the bracket is a; whatever is subtracted is b.
2
a² = (2x)² = 4x²
Square the whole first term — both the number and the letter.
3
2ab = 2(2x)(3) = 12x
Multiply 2 × a × b to get the middle term's size — the formula puts a minus in front of it here.
4
b² = (3)² = 9
Square the second term.
5
= 4x² − 12x + 9
Put the three pieces together in the pattern a² − 2ab + b².
WORKED EXAMPLE — WORKING BACKWARDS
25x² + px + 16 is a perfect square and p > 0. Find p.
1
25x² = (5x)², 16 = (4)²
Find the square roots of the first and last terms — these must be a and b.
2
So the expression is (5x + 4)²
If it really is a perfect square, it must be (a+b)² using the a and b you just found.
3
2(5x)(4) = 40x, so p = 40
The middle term of (a+b)² is 2ab — that's what gives you p.
Self-check: expand (x + 5)²
a=x, b=5 → x² + 2(x)(5) + 5² = x² + 10x + 25

STEP 4 OF 5

Expanding (x − a)(x + a)

This is the fastest pattern in the whole section — the middle terms always cancel, so there's nothing to combine at the end.

(x − a)(x + a) = x² − a²
WHY IT WORKS
1
(x − a)(x + a) = x² + ax − ax − a²
Expand normally — multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second.
2
+ax and −ax cancel
These two middle terms are equal and opposite, so they disappear completely.
3
= x² − a²
Only the two squared terms are left — that's the whole reason this pattern is worth memorising.
Self-check: expand (ax − by)(ax + by)
Treat ax as one block and by as another: (ax)² − (by)² = a²x² − b²y²

STEP 5 OF 5

Dividing algebraic expressions

Before dividing, always ask which of these three situations you're in — it decides your whole method.

Case 1 — the denominator divides every term

1
6x³−8x²y+4xy²+2x² ÷ 2x
Check: does 2x go evenly into each of the four terms on top? Yes — so divide them one at a time.
2
= 3x² − 4xy + 2y² + x
6x³÷2x=3x², −8x²y÷2x=−4xy, 4xy²÷2x=2y², 2x²÷2x=x.

Case 2 — the denominator is a bracket factor

1
6x² + 5xy + y² ÷ (2x + y)
Factorise the top first: 6x²+5xy+y² = (3x+y)(2x+y).
2
(3x+y)(2x+y) ÷ (2x+y) = 3x + y
The (2x+y) on top cancels with the (2x+y) on the bottom, leaving just the other factor.

Case 3 — long division

When nothing cancels neatly, divide term by term, the same way you would with numbers.

Divide (2x³ − 9x² + 10x − 3) by (x − 3): 2x² − 3x + 1 x − 3 ) 2x³ − 9x² + 10x − 3 2x³ − 6x² ─────────── −3x² + 10x −3x² + 9x ─────────── x − 3 x − 3 ───── 0 (2x³ − 9x² + 10x − 3) ÷ (x − 3) = 2x² − 3x + 1

Method: divide the leading term on top by the leading term of the bracket, multiply that answer by the whole bracket, subtract it from the top, then repeat with what's left over — until nothing remains.

Self-check: simplify (12a²b) ÷ (3ab)
12÷3=4, a²÷a=a, b÷b=1 → 4a

SECTION 1.5 · BINOMIAL EXPANSIONS

Binomial expansions — using the formula

Expanding (x+y) to a high power by hand is slow, so instead you match the expression to a ready-made pattern and just plug numbers in. The whole process is: identify x, y and n; write out the general pattern; then substitute and simplify.

(x + y)ⁿ = (n,0)xⁿ + (n,1)xⁿ⁻¹y + (n,2)xⁿ⁻²y² + … + (n,n)yⁿ
(n,r) means "n choose r" — found using the nCr button on a calculator, or read straight off Pascal's triangle

A few things worth knowing about (n,r): (n,0) and (n,n) are always 1; (n,1) is always n; and (n,r) always equals (n, n−r) — so you never need to calculate more than half of a row.

WORKED EXAMPLE
Find the first 3 terms of the expansion of (1 − 5y)⁸
1
Match to (x+y)ⁿ: x = 1, y = −5y, n = 8
Line the expression up against the general pattern so you know exactly what to substitute.
2
(8,0)x⁸ + (8,1)x⁷y + (8,2)x⁶y² + …
Write out the first three terms of the general pattern — since x = 1, every power of x just disappears.
3
(8,0) = 1, (8,1) = 8, (8,2) = 28
Work out the three binomial coefficients — by calculator or Pascal's triangle.
4
= 1 + 8(−5y) + 28(−5y)² = 1 − 40y + 700y²
Substitute y = −5y into each term and simplify, keeping track of the negative sign.
WORKED EXAMPLE — FINDING ONE PARTICULAR TERM
Find the 4th term of the expansion of (3a + b)⁷
1
Match: x = 3a, y = b, n = 7
Same first move as always — identify the pieces before touching any numbers.
2
Term number = r + 1, so the 4th term needs r = 3
The general term is (n,r)xⁿ⁻ʳyʳ, and counting starts at r = 0 — so the 4th term is always one behind, at r = 3.
3
(7,3)(3a)⁴b³ = 35 × 81a⁴ × b³
Substitute r = 3 into the general term and evaluate (7,3) = 35 and (3a)⁴ = 81a⁴.
4
= 2835a⁴b³
Multiply the pieces together for the final term.
Self-check: expand (a + 2b)⁴ fully using the Pascal's triangle row 1 4 6 4 1
a⁴ + 4a³(2b) + 6a²(2b)² + 4a(2b)³ + (2b)⁴ = a⁴ + 8a³b + 24a²b² + 32ab³ + 16b⁴

SECTION 1.6 · ALGEBRAIC IDENTITIES

Algebraic identities

An identity is an equation that's true for every single value of x, not just one or two. Because of that, the coefficients of matching powers of x on each side must be exactly equal — and that fact is the key to unlocking unknown letters.

If two expressions are equal for all values of x, the coefficients of every matching power of x must be equal.

Finding unknown coefficients

Find a and b given that (2x + a)² = 4x² + 12x + b for all values of x
1
Expand the left-hand side: 4x² + 4ax + a²
You can't compare anything until both sides are written as full polynomials.
2
Compare the x coefficients: 4a = 12 → a = 3
The x² terms already match (4x² on both sides), so the next useful comparison is the x term.
3
Compare the constants: a² = b → b = 9
With a already known, substitute it into the constant-term comparison to get b.

Building partial fractions from an identity

The process: write the right-hand side as a single fraction, then, since the denominators already match, the top lines (numerators) must be equal for every x. Substituting a smart value of x can make one unknown vanish completely.

Given 1 / [(x+1)(x−2)] = A/(x+1) + B/(x−2), find A and B
1
Combine the right side: [A(x−2) + B(x+1)] / [(x+1)(x−2)]
Put both fractions over the same common denominator so they can be compared directly to the left side.
2
Since the denominators match: 1 = A(x−2) + B(x+1)
If two fractions with the same denominator are equal, their numerators must be equal too.
3
Let x = 2: 1 = 3B → B = 1/3
Choosing x = 2 makes the A(x−2) term disappear completely, leaving only B to solve for.
4
Let x = −1: 1 = −3A → A = −1/3
Choosing x = −1 makes the B(x+1) term disappear instead, isolating A.

Identities and factors

When one polynomial divides exactly into another, there's no remainder — and setting that remainder to zero (since it must hold for every x) gives you a relationship between the unknown letters.

If x² − ax + b is a factor of x³ + 2ax² + 4bx + c, then: (i) b = −a² and (ii) c = 3ab

SECTION 1.7 · MANIPULATING FORMULAE

Manipulating formulae

Making a letter "the subject" of a formula means getting it standing completely alone on one side. The process is always: clear anything blocking it — a square root, a fraction, a bracket — then isolate it, exactly like solving an equation.

If v² = u² + 2as, express a in terms of v, u and s
1
Move u² to the other side: 2as = v² − u²
Get everything that isn't "a" onto the opposite side first.
2
Divide both sides by 2s: a = (v² − u²) / 2s
a is being multiplied by 2s, so dividing by 2s leaves a on its own.
A TRICKIER ONE — WITH A SQUARE ROOT
If √[(x+y)/(x−y)] = 1/2, express y in terms of x, then find y when x = 5
1
Square both sides: (x+y)/(x−y) = 1/4
Squaring removes the square root, since √a squared is just a.
2
Cross-multiply: 4(x+y) = x − y
Multiply both sides by 4(x−y) to clear both fractions at once.
3
Expand: 4x + 4y = x − y
Multiply out the bracket before moving anything else around.
4
Gather y terms on one side: 5y = −3x
Move all the y's to the left and all the x's to the right, changing signs as terms cross the equals sign.
5
Divide by 5: y = −3x/5. When x = 5, y = −3
Once y is isolated, plug in x = 5 to get the numerical answer.
Self-check: given x = (t+4)/(3t+1), find t in terms of x
Step 1: multiply both sides by (3t+1): x(3t+1) = t+4
Step 2: expand: 3tx + x = t + 4
Step 3: gather t terms: 3tx − t = 4 − x
Step 4: factorise: t(3x−1) = 4−x
Step 5: divide: t = (4−x)/(3x−1)

SECTION 1.8 · ALGEBRAIC PATTERNS

Algebraic patterns

Every number pattern is checked the same way: look at how much each term changes by (the 1st difference). If that's a constant number, the pattern is linear. If it isn't constant, look at how that changes (the 2nd difference) — if that's constant, the pattern is quadratic.

1st difference constant → linear pattern, f(x) = ax + b
2nd difference constant → quadratic pattern, f(x) = ax² + bx + c, where 2a = the 2nd difference
WORKED EXAMPLE — LINEAR
Find f(x) for the pattern −2, 1, 4, 7, …
1
1st differences: 3, 3, 3 — constant
Since every term goes up by exactly the same amount, this must be linear.
2
a = 3 (the constant 1st difference)
In a linear pattern, the slope a is simply how much each term increases by.
3
b = −2 (the starting term, at x = 0)
b is just the very first value in the pattern.
4
f(x) = 3x − 2
Put a and b into f(x) = ax + b.
WORKED EXAMPLE — QUADRATIC
A matchstick pattern gives 4, 10, 18, 28, … Find f(x), then the 10th pattern.
1
1st differences: 6, 8, 10 — not constant
Since these keep changing, the pattern isn't linear.
2
2nd differences: 2, 2 — constant
A constant 2nd difference confirms this is a quadratic pattern.
3
2a = 2 → a = 1, and c = f(0) = 4
a comes from the constant 2nd difference; c is just the very first term of the pattern.
4
f(1) = a+b+c = 10 → 1+b+4=10 → b = 5
Use the second term of the pattern (f(1)=10) together with the a and c you already know to solve for b.
5
f(x) = x² + 5x + 4, so f(9) = 81+45+4 = 130
The pattern starts at x=0, so the 10th pattern is x=9 — substitute and evaluate.

SECTION 1.9 · SOLVING EQUATIONS

Solving linear equations

When an equation has fractions, the process is always the same: find the lowest common denominator (LCD) of every fraction, rewrite each term over it, then drop the denominators completely and solve what's left like an ordinary equation.

Solve: (2t−3)/5 + 1/20 = (t−1)/4
1
LCD of 5, 20 and 4 is 20
Find the smallest number that 5, 20 and 4 all divide into evenly.
2
4(2t−3)/20 + 1/20 = 5(t−1)/20
Rewrite each fraction so every one has 20 as its denominator.
3
4(2t−3) + 1 = 5(t−1)
Since every term now shares the same denominator, it can be dropped entirely — multiply the whole equation by 20.
4
8t − 12 + 1 = 5t − 5
Expand both brackets.
5
3t = 6
Collect all the t terms on one side and all the plain numbers on the other.
6
t = 2
Divide both sides by the coefficient of t.

SECTION 1.10 · SIMULTANEOUS EQUATIONS

Solving simultaneous linear equations

Two linear equations in x and y usually cross at exactly one point — the one pair of values that satisfies both at the same time. Two reliable methods: substitution (rearrange one equation and plug it into the other) and elimination (scale the equations so a variable cancels when you add or subtract them).

METHOD 1 — SUBSTITUTION
Solve: 3x − y = 1 and x − 2y = −8
1
Rearrange the easier equation for x: x = −8 + 2y
Pick whichever variable has a coefficient of 1 — it's the least work to isolate.
2
Substitute into the other equation: 3(−8+2y) − y = 1
Replace every x in the remaining equation with the expression you just found.
3
Expand and solve: −24+6y−y=1 → 5y=25 → y=5
Now there's only one variable left, so solve it exactly as you would any linear equation.
4
Back-substitute: x = −8+2(5) = 2. Point: (2, 5)
Plug y back into the rearranged equation from step 1 to find x.
METHOD 2 — ELIMINATION
Solve: 2x − 5y = 9 and 3x + 2y = 4
1
×3 the first, ×2 the second: 6x−15y=27 and 6x+4y=8
Scale each equation so that x has the exact same coefficient (6x) in both.
2
Subtract: −19y = 19 → y = −1
Subtracting cancels the matching 6x terms, leaving one equation in y alone.
3
Substitute into 2x−5y=9: 2x+5=9 → x=2. Point: (2, −1)
Plug y back into either original equation to find x.

With three variables (x, y, z), the idea is the same but in two stages: eliminate the same variable from two different pairs of equations to get two equations in two unknowns, solve those as above, then substitute back to find the third variable.

SECTION 1.10 · WORD PROBLEMS IN CONTEXT

Turning word problems into equations

This is the hardest-feeling skill in the chapter, but it's really just careful reading. The process: name your unknowns clearly, then translate the problem one sentence at a time into an equation — don't try to do it all in your head at once. Once you have as many equations as unknowns, solve exactly as before.

WORKED EXAMPLE
50c, 20c and 10c coins are collected from a machine. The total value is €32. Twice the number of 20c coins, added to the number of 10c coins, equals three times the number of 50c coins. Four times the number of 50c coins, added to the number of 10c coins, equals six times the number of 20c coins. Find the number of each coin.
1
Let x = number of 50c, y = number of 20c, z = number of 10c
Always name your unknowns explicitly before writing a single equation — it stops you losing track of what each letter means.
2
"Total value is €32" → 50x + 20y + 10z = 3200
Convert euro to cents (×100) so it matches the coin values, and add up each coin's total contribution.
3
"Twice the 20c, plus the 10c, equals three times the 50c" → 2y + z = 3x
Translate the sentence phrase by phrase, left to right, exactly as it's written.
4
"Four times the 50c, plus the 10c, equals six times the 20c" → 4x + z = 6y
Same approach again — this gives the third equation needed for three unknowns.
5
Rearrange all three into standard form and solve as a 3-variable system
From here it's the same elimination process as Section 1.10 — eliminate one letter from two different pairs, then back-substitute.
6
(x, y, z) = (40, 35, 50)
40 fifty-cent coins, 35 twenty-cent coins, and 50 ten-cent coins.
Self-check: the sum of two numbers is 26. Five times the smaller number taken from four times the larger number gives 5. Let x be the bigger number and y the smaller. Write two equations and solve.
Equation 1: x + y = 26
Equation 2: 4x − 5y = 5
From eq 1: x = 26 − y. Substitute: 4(26−y) − 5y = 5 → 104 − 9y = 5 → y = 11, so x = 15.

PULLING IT ALL TOGETHER

Chapter review — mixed practice

You've now covered every section in Algebra 1. Exam-style questions mix ideas from across the whole chapter, so the hardest part is often recognising which method a question wants before you even start writing.

WORKED EXAMPLE — COMBINING PATTERNS & SIMULTANEOUS EQUATIONS
The curve y = ax² + bx + c passes through (0, 1), (2, 9) and (4, 41). Find a, b and c.
1
Substitute each point into y = ax²+bx+c
Every point on the curve must satisfy the equation, so each one gives you a linear equation in a, b and c.
2
(0,1): c = 1
Substituting x = 0 is always the easiest point to use — every term with an x in it disappears.
3
(2,9): 4a+2b+c=9 → 2a+b=4 (using c=1)
Substitute the point, then immediately simplify using the value of c you already know.
4
(4,41): 16a+4b+c=41 → 4a+b=10 (using c=1)
Same again for the third point — you now have two equations in just a and b.
5
Subtract: 2a=6 → a=3, then b=4−2(3)=−2
With c already known, this has become an ordinary two-variable simultaneous system.
6
f(x) = 3x² − 2x + 1
Collect a, b and c together into the final quadratic.
